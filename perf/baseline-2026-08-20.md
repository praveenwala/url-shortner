# URL shortener load baseline — 2026-08-20

Measurement only. Nothing was tuned, and no architecture decision was taken here; that is the
point of a baseline. Reproduce with `perf/run_baseline.sh`.

## Verdict

| Target | Measured (nominal) | Measured (hot skew) | Result |
|--------|-------------------|---------------------|--------|
| 100 redirects/sec sustained | 100.0 rps | 100.0 rps | **PASS** |
| p95 < 150 ms | **6.82 ms** | **6.62 ms** | **PASS** — ~22× headroom |
| p99 < 400 ms | **10.84 ms** | **11.64 ms** | **PASS** — ~34× headroom |
| error rate | 0.000000 | 0.000000 | **PASS** |

**Redis is not justified by these measurements.** Research R13 makes a cache tier reachable
only when the budget is breached *and* the breach is attributed to datastore access *and*
cheaper rungs are exhausted. The budget is not breached — the first condition fails, so the
ladder does not begin.

| Duration | 120 s per profile, after a 6 s warm-up excluded from all figures |

**No Redis. No application or schema change was made for the benchmark.** The synchronous
analytics path was not bypassed: every successful redirect still updates the counter and
inserts a `redirect_event` in the same durable transaction before the 302 is issued.

## Methodology

* **Open loop.** Requests are scheduled at a fixed 100 rps rather than issued as responses
  arrive. A closed-loop tool would silently lower the offered load when the service slows,
  hiding the degradation the test exists to find.
* **Redirects are not followed.** The endpoint returns 302 by design; following it would
  measure example.com rather than this service.
* **Generator overhead is measured and reported separately** as `scheduler_lag_ms` — the gap
  between a request's scheduled and actual send. It stayed at p95 ≤ 0.81 ms in both profiles,
  so client lag is roughly 12% of the measured service latency and is not confused with it.
* **Seeding bypasses the API deliberately.** Creating 100k links through `POST /v1/links`
  would take longer than the benchmark and would pre-load the counters being measured.

## Analysis, in the order the decision ladder requires

1. **Query and index behaviour** — the lookup is a primary-key hit averaging 0.05 ms and is
   the *cheapest* of the three statements. No index problem exists to fix.
2. **Connection pool** — 1–2 active connections against a pool of 16. Nowhere near saturation;
   pool sizing is not a factor at this load.
3. **Transaction duration** — the three statements total ~0.24 ms of database time per
   redirect against ~5.4 ms end-to-end, so roughly 95% of the latency is application, HTTP,
   and loopback, not the database transaction.
4. **Hot-row counter contention** — **present and measurable, but not material.** Concentrating
   70% of traffic on 5 rows raised the counter `UPDATE` mean from 0.072 ms to 0.088 ms (+22%)
   and produced a single 29.21 ms outlier and one observed lock wait in 24 samples. It did not
   move p95 (6.82 → 6.62 ms, i.e. *lower* than nominal, within run-to-run noise) and moved p99
   by 0.8 ms. The recorded hot-row risk is real and now quantified: at 100 rps it costs tens of
   microseconds of mean UPDATE time.
5. **Event insert cost** — the `redirect_event` insert is the single largest database
   contributor in both profiles (1.13–1.17 s total, ~0.095 ms mean). If anything were ever to
   be optimised first, this is what the evidence points at — *not* the counter update that the
   recorded risk anticipated. Worth noting, not worth acting on at 22× headroom.
6. **Architectural alternatives** — not reached. The ladder's first rung is not exhausted
   because there is no breach to explain.

## Limitations

* **Single-host measurement.** Application, database, and load generator share one machine, so
  results include loopback and co-tenancy effects. Real deployment latency would add network
  time and remove co-tenancy contention; the direction of the net effect is not predictable
  from this run.
* **The two-role privilege model was not exercised.** The benchmark runs as a single database
  role so Flyway can migrate on startup. Privileges do not change query plans, so this does not
  affect the latency figures, but it means the production `ops/db/provision.sh` split is
  untested here.
* **Hikari pool metrics were not read directly.** Actuator exposure is limited to
  `health,info`, so pool usage is inferred from `pg_stat_activity` rather than from the pool.
* **Skew shape is one of many.** 70% onto 5 links is a plausible hot-link pattern, not the
  worst case. A single link taking 100% of traffic would contend harder; that was not measured.
* **Duration.** 120 s per profile is enough to leave startup behind but is not a soak test;
  connection churn, vacuum behaviour, and table growth over hours are unmeasured.
* **Exactness confirmed under load.** After all runs, `redirect_event` rows (26,801) equalled
  the sum of `redirect_count` (26,801) exactly — SC-006 held under concurrent load, including
  the skewed profile.
